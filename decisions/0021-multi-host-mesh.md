# ADR-0021 — Daemons that link: a mesh across hosts

**Status:** Accepted
**Date:** 2026-10-17
**Accepted:** 2026-10-17, by the Architect.
**Amended:** 2026-10-18: a LAN announcement is not signed, since a node key is an X25519 key and cannot sign; it is a hint of where a peer's link endpoint is, acted on only for a key already in the allowlist, which the sealed handshake then authenticates, and the handshakes announcements cause are rate-limited per key. Item 2 is restated to say so, and which trust a peer configured by its key alone is given.
**Amended:** 2026-10-18: forwarding past one hop goes through relays only. A daemon configured as a relay forwards once more what one peer sent it, and its interest summaries and node announcements cover its other peers; a receiver accepts traffic relayed for another daemon only from a peer it trusts as a relay, and such traffic's origin is as trustworthy as that relay. Item 5 is restated, item 9 added, and the order of work updated.

## Context

Everything a daemon does today stays on its host. A client attaches to one daemon, which publishes to one hub; `PulseHub::route_envelope` broadcasts, and `send_envelope` ignores its destination (`mesh/mesh-hub-runtime/src/lib.rs`). A process on another host reaches a hub only through point-to-point UDP (`pulse-wire-udp`) that the application wires itself, as the 200-worker run of 2026-10-16 did. The pieces a mesh needs exist apart and unused: the capability Hello (`mesh-capability`, pre-integration), context ids for the high half of a Pulse's scope (`mesh-context`, pre-integration), node ids, content-addressed envelope ids, health tracking (#76, #99), opt-in congestion control (#85) and multicast (#94). What none of them settles is how two daemons find each other, trust each other, and decide which envelopes cross between them.

This ADR builds on ADR-0018, the security model: a link between hosts carries other parties' traffic, so it is built on ADR-0018's authenticated, sealed transport or not at all.

## Decision

1. **A daemon may link to peer daemons.** A link is one ADR-0018 session between two daemons, authenticated by their node keys, carrying envelopes and a small set of control messages (interest, health, Hello). A daemon serves its local clients as today; links are an addition, configured, off by default.
2. **Peers are configured, and optionally announced on a LAN.** A daemon links only to peers named in configuration, each by the public key the link must authenticate, and either by an address too or by that key alone. A daemon may also announce its link endpoint on a LAN multicast group (#94): a small plaintext datagram naming its node key's public half and its link port, the address being the datagram's source. The announcement is not signed, since the node key is an X25519 key, which agrees secrets and cannot sign (ADR-0018, item 6). It is only a hint of where a peer's link endpoint is: a daemon acts on it only for a key already in its allowlist, and the sealed IK handshake then authenticates that key, so a forged announcement can at worst make a daemon attempt handshakes that fail. Those attempts are rate-limited per key, one every five seconds, so that forged announcements cannot make a daemon flood handshakes. A peer configured with an address keeps it, whatever announcements say, and is admitted only from that address's IP; a peer configured by its key alone takes its address from announcements and is admitted from any address its handshake comes from, its key authenticated all the same. There is no discovery service and no global directory in this design. *(Amended 2026-10-18: it said an announcement is a Hello signed by the node key.)*
3. **Origins are qualified by their daemon.** A client's node id today is a hash of the name it attached with, unique only on its daemon. Across links an origin is the pair of the daemon's key fingerprint and the local name (`name@daemon`), so two hosts' clients of the same name stay distinct; locally nothing changes.
4. **Interest decides what crosses.** Each daemon tells its peers, over the link, a summary of what its subscribers ask for: the exact channels and type names, and the glob prefixes before the first wildcard. A send is forwarded to a peer whose summary may match it, and only then; the peer matches it exactly against its own subscribers as today. A summary is replaced whole when it changes, at most a few times a second, so a busy subscriber set costs a bounded control rate rather than one message per subscribe.
5. **One hop, or two through a relay; more with a limit and a memory.** A daemon forwards to its direct peers, and a send crosses at most one relay on its way (item 9). A forward carries a hop limit in the Pulse's `chain_step`, decremented at each daemon and dropped at zero, which gives that reserved field the meaning its first documentation had: an envelope leaves its origin with a limit of two, so a relay may pass it on once and no daemon after the relay forwards it again. Each daemon keeps a bounded cache of recently forwarded envelopes, keyed on the envelope's id, its origin and its send stamp, so an envelope reaching it twice by two paths (directly and through a relay) is delivered once while a repeated send is not mistaken for a copy. Forwarding through any number of hops in an arbitrary topology is not decided here: propagating interest around loops without leaving stale interest behind is a routing design of its own, for a later ADR if a deployment needs it.
6. **A destination is honoured.** `send_envelope`'s `dest` becomes a qualified node id; the daemon delivers locally when the node is its own client, forwards to the peer that announced the node otherwise, and refuses with an error naming the node when no peer has.
7. **Health crosses links.** A link carries a heartbeat; a peer daemon's own state, and the states of its clients that local watchers ask for (`health` subscriptions), arrive as the same `health` notices, with qualified node ids. A link that falls silent past its limit is closed and its peer's nodes are reported `detached`.
8. **Delivery stays what each transport gives.** Across a link, envelopes are delivered at most once, in no promised order, with the link's NACK, FEC and congestion control (#85) as configured; nothing here adds a broker, persistence or retries beyond the transport's.
9. **Relays carry traffic one hop further.** A daemon started as a relay (`--link-relay`) forwards an envelope it received from one peer, with hop limit left, to its other peers whose interest may match it, never back to the peer it came from, decrementing the hop limit. Its interest summary to each peer is its own subscribers' interest together with the interest its other non-relay peers summarised to it, the receiving peer's own excluded, so a peer sends the relay what the relay's other peers want; relays do not aggregate one another's summaries, which keeps every summary bounded to one relay's neighbourhood and no loop can feed interest back into itself. Its node announcements likewise name its other peers' nodes, with itself as the way to them, and destinations and health notices for those nodes pass through it the same way. A daemon prefers a direct route to a node over a relay's. A receiver refuses a forward whose origin is not the sending peer's own daemon unless it trusts that peer as a relay (`--link-trust-relay PUBKEY`); a relay is authenticated as itself by the sealed session, but the origin it reports for traffic it relays is only as trustworthy as the relay, since nothing signs an envelope end to end.

## Consequences

- **Cost.** A forwarded send pays one sealed datagram per interested peer; an uninterested peer costs nothing per send, only its summary. The control traffic is bounded by the summary rate, not the subscription rate.
- **Partitions are visible, not hidden.** A link that drops reports its peer's nodes `detached`; envelopes sent while it is down are not stored for later. A design that buffers across partitions is a separate decision.
- **Large envelopes cross only once fragmentation exists** (P1-6, which follows ADR-0018's frame format); until then a link carries envelopes up to the sealed frame's limit and refuses larger ones with an error.
- **The two pre-integration crates find their use.** `mesh-capability`'s Hello is the first message on a link (inside the session, so it cannot be stripped), and `mesh-context`'s ids are how a deployment names the scopes its daemons share; both lose their pre-integration status when the link code lands.
- **Not decided here:** a directory or discovery beyond configuration and LAN announcement, multi-tenant isolation between deployments that share daemons, and quotas per peer.

## Order of work

Once ADR-0018's sealed UDP (L2-6) and capability handshake (L2-9) land: links with configured peers and one-hop interest forwarding, then destinations, then health across links, then LAN announcement, then relays (item 9). General multi-hop forwarding, if a deployment needs it, follows its own ADR.
